The R row

わ, を and ん

Things to know

The R sound sits between R and L

Japanese “r” is made by lightly tapping the tip of your tongue behind your upper teeth, a bit like the “tt” in the American “butter”. It is neither an English “r” nor a clear “l”.

を is the object particle

を is pronounced “o” and is used almost only as a grammar particle. It marks what the action is done to: ほんを よむ (hon o yomu) means “read a book”.

ん is a syllable on its own

ん is the only hiragana that is not a vowel or a consonant+vowel. It's a nasal “n” that takes a full beat, as in にほん (ni-ho-n).

Words you can read now

  • さくらsakuracherry blossom
  • くるまkurumacar
  • ほんhonbook
  • みかんmikanmandarin orange
  • わたしwatashiI, me
  • からてkaratekarate
  • いろirocolour
  • ろくrokusix
  • れもんremonlemon
  • にほんnihonJapan

Notes

  • わたしは ほんを よむ — “I read a book”. Every character in this sentence is now readable. Try reading it out loud, with は as “wa” and を as “o”.
  • る and ろ are close, and so are ね, れ and わ. Compare them in the charts above.
  • You now know all 46 basic hiragana! Next up are the sound-change marks, which multiply what you can read.

How to practise

  • Say each sound out loud as you read it. Reading silently is much slower for building memory.
  • Write each new character about ten times on paper, following the stroke order from a reliable chart. Writing helps you notice the small differences between similar characters.
  • Cover the romaji column and try to read the characters, then check yourself. This is what the quiz at the end of every lesson does too.
